# What is known of what makes a book good

Before anything was asked of Jev, what those who know have held or found was gathered from five sides: criticism and theory, from Aristotle and Longinus to Eagleton; the craft, as writers, editors and workshops teach it; the institutions that judge, prizes and their juries, marking rubrics, librarians; those who measure texts and readers; and the study of reading for pleasure. The questions put to Jev, the two valuations and the way they were checked all come from it, and much of what Jev then showed had been said beforehand. The working reports copy out others' rubrics at length and are not in the repo: this is what they come to. The working reports were written by language models set to research, so every figure and quotation below was checked again against its source, opened for the purpose, before being written here; the sources are at the end.

## Where they agree

Across schools and centuries the same short list comes back. Most of it can be seen in a passage; the last three take the whole book.

## Where they do not

- **Show, don't tell** is a workshop rule that Le Guin, Francine Prose and Wayne Booth all argue against, and the line about the glint of light on broken glass, given as Chekhov's rule, is a later paraphrase of advice in a letter.
- **Difficulty.** Long sentences, density and the unforeseen word are marks of the prose that prizes reward and what puts most readers off. "Hard to follow" cannot simply count against merit.
- **Polish is not merit.** Longinus puts the great and uneven over the flawless and small; Borges calls the perfect page the most precarious of all. A book is more than the absence of faults, and a mean of clean passages may rank books wrongly.
- **Nobody weights.** None of the institutions looked at gives its criteria weights, and the Newbery terms say why: a book is to be distinguished "in all of the elements pertinent to it", and need not be in every one.
- **No list of criteria gives the verdict.** Eagleton holds that there are public criteria of excellence in fiction as in golf, that they guide a judgement and do not make it, and that among the candidates, "depth of insight, truth-to-life, formal unity, universal appeal, moral complexity, verbal inventiveness, imaginative vision", not one holds for every good book.

## Two valuations, not one

"You can enjoy books you do not admire and admire books you do not enjoy", says Eagleton, and it is no whim of readers on a site of ratings. Bourdieu describes two aesthetics, one that puts form over function and keeps its distance, one that asks art to go on from life. The psychology of entertainment tells enjoyment, which is fun and suspense, from appreciation, which is being moved and made to think, and finds them to be two things and not the ends of one: on films seen for an evening the two barely go together, with correlations of .04 to .09, and on a classic seen in a class on film they do, .50 to .79. And when some 14,000 Dutch readers rated 401 novels twice, from 1 to 7, as literary and as good, the two ratings of a book agreed only in part, with a correlation of 0.6.

The same survey says which of the two is in the text. From the words of a novel, how literary its readers hold it is predicted to 61 hundredths of its variance, and how good they hold it, with the same means, to 22 to 34; and the kind of book it is, its genre, predicts 58 by itself. On a site of ratings, of 566,000 ratings of 50,000 books, what a rating owes to the book is 3.5 hundredths of its variance and what it owes to who the reader is, 30. Measures of worth fall in two families that do not agree, those of the canon, syllabuses and anthologies, and those of the public, ratings and sales. Those who serve readers, librarians, do not give a book a mark at all: Nancy Pearl says by which door a reader goes into it, story, character, setting or language, and holds _Cien años de soledad_ to be mostly language.

Some things count for one valuation and against the other: easy language, a swift pace, the conventions of a genre fulfilled. Fluency pleases, "the more fluently perceivers can process an object, the more positive their aesthetic response", and what is finished is not what is prized: of those who began them on one reading device, of British readers 62 in a hundred finished a romance and 44 the novel that took that year's Pulitzer.

## Faults are easier than virtues

The craft is a literature of faults. The Turkey City Lexicon, a workshop's catalogue, names them by the dozen: the white room, the infodump, the character who tells another what both know. Faults tell the bad from the decent, and published books have few of the ones that fill a slush pile; and a question about a fault is one a model can answer of a passage, which a question about greatness is not.

## Many weak questions

Twelve stories from _The New Yorker_ and thirty-six by language models were put to experts with fourteen closed tests of creative writing. On any one test the experts agreed with each other moderately, a kappa of 0.27 to 0.66 and 0.41 on average; on how many tests a story passed, strongly, a correlation of 0.69; and the stories of the magazine passed 85 in a hundred of the tests, those of the models 9 to 30. It is the case for asking many narrow things and adding them up. The same study had three language models of 2023 apply the tests, and they agreed with the experts no better than chance, kappas of −0.01 to 0.04.

## Machines as judges

Language models set to judge writing are moved by what is beside the point, and the ways are catalogued, twelve in one study: the longer answer, the place of an answer in a pair, an authority cited, what others are said to think, the feeling in the wording. A workshop's taxonomy of what is wrong with the prose of language models has purple prose in it, and a public benchmark of their fiction marks it down by name. The standards themselves move slowly: what told a reviewed volume of poetry from an unreviewed one held for a century, 1820 to 1919, a model getting it right 79 times in a hundred.
